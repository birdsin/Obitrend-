Prepare a Garment Photo for an AI Fashion Workflow

A practical step-by-step guide to photographing clothing references with clear shape, colour, texture and construction details.

Start with the purpose of the reference

A useful garment photograph should communicate what the item actually looks like before creative styling begins. Decide whether the image is intended to show the complete silhouette, a particular detail, or both. If the garment has distinctive straps, collars, pockets, pleats or surface decoration, plan how those features will be visible.

Choose a simple setup

Use a clean, uncluttered background that contrasts with the garment. Place the item on a hanger, mannequin or flat surface according to its shape. Smooth large folds without stretching the fabric or hiding natural construction. Keep the camera level and avoid wide-angle distortion by stepping back slightly and using a normal camera view.

Use even light

Soft daylight near a window is often a practical starting point. Avoid mixing daylight with strongly coloured indoor bulbs. Harsh direct sunlight can create deep shadows and make dark fabrics lose detail. For black clothing, expose carefully so seams and texture remain visible; for white clothing, avoid blown highlights.

Photograph the details that matter

Take a full front view and, where useful, a back view. Add close-ups of the neckline, sleeves, waistband, closures, stitching, print, embroidery or fabric texture. Keep each image sharp and make sure the detail is not hidden by hands, tags or accessories.

Check colour and focus

Clean the camera lens and tap to focus on the garment. Compare the photo with the real item under neutral light. Filters, portrait effects and heavy editing can shift colour or soften edges. Keep an untouched reference copy so later edits can be compared with the original.

Before uploading

  • The whole garment is visible in at least one clear image.
  • Important design features are not covered or cropped.
  • The lighting shows the real colour as closely as possible.
  • Fabric texture and seams are in focus.
  • The background does not compete with the clothing.
  • You have permission to use the photograph and any visible branding.

How to use this guide

Use the checklist as a starting point, then adapt it to the garment, audience and channel you are working with. Keep a record of the decisions that affect the final image so your next shoot or creative brief is easier to repeat.

AI image reminder: generated fashion images are visual concepts, not proof of a product's exact colour, construction, fit or material. Compare outputs with the real item before publishing product claims.

A repeatable garment-reference workflow

1. Prepare the garment without changing it

Before taking pictures, check that the item is clean, dry and presented in its normal shape. Fasten closures as they would ordinarily be worn. Arrange sleeves, legs and hems naturally rather than pulling the garment into an unrealistic outline. If the item is designed to drape, allow it to drape; if it is structured, show its structure without forcing it flat.

2. Choose the display method deliberately

A flat-lay photograph is useful for showing the overall pattern and arrangement of panels. A hanger can show the natural drop of a shirt, dress or jacket, provided the hanger does not cover the neckline or shoulders. A mannequin can communicate shape and fit, but make sure its proportions do not stretch the garment. For complex pieces, use more than one method and label the views clearly.

3. Control the background and camera position

Leave a visible margin around the clothing so the outline is easy to distinguish. Keep the camera sensor parallel to the main plane of the garment where possible; shooting from an extreme angle can make one side appear larger. Use the main camera lens rather than an ultra-wide setting. Keep the camera at a consistent distance for comparison shots and avoid digital zoom if it reduces detail.

4. Make a small reference set

A practical reference set can include a full front view, full back view, side view when shape matters, and close-ups of distinctive features. Name files descriptively, such as “front”, “back”, “neckline-detail” and “fabric-closeup”. This helps a designer, photographer or creative team understand what each image is intended to show. Do not assume that one photograph can communicate every construction detail.

Common photo problems and how to correct them

  • Colour cast: if a white shirt looks blue or a neutral fabric looks orange, move to one consistent light source and turn off mixed-colour lamps.
  • Lost black detail: move the garment toward soft, brighter light and check that the camera is not underexposing the shadows.
  • White fabric with no texture: reduce exposure slightly or reposition the garment so highlights retain detail.
  • Motion blur: stabilise the phone against a support, use a timer and take several frames.
  • Distracting wrinkles: steam or arrange the garment according to its care instructions; do not digitally erase seams or folds that are part of its real construction.
  • Scale distortion: move farther away and use a standard lens view rather than placing the phone very close to the garment.

What to record with the photo

Keep a short reference note with the garment name or internal product code, the date photographed, the known colour description, material information from a reliable label or supplier, and any details that are easy to confuse. Record uncertainty rather than guessing. For example, if the exact fibre composition is unknown, say “composition not confirmed” instead of inventing a material claim.

Using the photo in an AI workflow

Choose the clearest image as the primary reference and use detail photographs to verify important features. State which attributes must remain unchanged, such as neckline, sleeve length, waistband, print placement and fastenings. Treat the output as a draft: generative systems can alter small details even when given a clear reference. Compare the result with the original before using it in a catalogue, advert or customer-facing listing.

Practical exercise: photograph one garment using a flat lay and a hanger. Compare how each method communicates its silhouette, neckline and fabric. Choose the view that answers the most important question for your intended audience, then add detail shots for anything it does not show.
